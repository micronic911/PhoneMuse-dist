Introduction, specs, unboxing

Introduction

Samsung expanded its foldable lineup in a major way this year. Admittedly, the naming could have been better selected, but we now have a brand-new form factor in the Z Fold8. The old, well-established Fold is also alive and well, but it is now called the Z Fold8 Ultra.

In isolation, the new naming makes sense. The traditional form-factor (the Z Fold8 Ultra) is the true foldable flagship - the one with all the bells and whistles. The regular Z Fold8 cuts a few corners to achieve the light, slender body, with the camera setup being the most obvious one.

Samsung Galaxy Z Fold8 specs at a glance:

  • Body: 161.4x123.9x4.5mm, 201g; Glass front (Gorilla Glass Victus Ceramic 3) (folded), plastic front (unfolded), glass back (Gorilla Glass Victus 2), Advanced Armor aluminum frame, IP48 dust and water resistant (dust > 1mm; immersible up to 1.5m for 30 min).
  • Display: 7.60" Foldable Dynamic LTPO AMOLED 2X, 120Hz, HDR10+, 3000 nits (peak), 1828x2448px resolution, 12.05:9 aspect ratio, 403ppi; Flex Titanium technology, Cover display:, Dynamic LTPO AMOLED 2X, 120Hz, Corning Gorilla Glass Ceramic 3, 5.5 inches, 1248 x 1972 pixels, 428 PPI.
  • Chipset: Qualcomm SM8850-1-AD Snapdragon 8 Elite Gen 5 (3 nm).
  • Memory: 256GB 12GB RAM, 512GB 12GB RAM, 1TB 12GB RAM, 1TB 16GB RAM; UFS 4.x.
  • OS/Software: Android 17, up to 7 major Android upgrades, One UI 9.
  • Rear camera: Wide (main): 50 MP, f/1.8, 23mm, 1/1.57", 1.0µm, dual pixel PDAF, OIS; Ultra wide angle: 50 MP, f/1.9, 120-degree, 1/2.5", 0.7µm, dual pixel PDAF.
  • Front camera: Wide (main): 10 MP, f/2.2, 18mm, 1/3.0", 1.12µm; Wide (main): 10 MP, f/2.2, 24mm, 1/3.0", 1.12µm.
  • Video capture: Rear camera: 8K@30fps, 4K@30/60/120fps, 1080p@30/60/120/240fps, gyro-EIS, OIS; Front camera: 4K@30/60fps, 1080p@30/60fps, gyro-EIS.
  • Battery: 4800mAh; 45W wired, QC2.0, 63% in 30 min, 20W wireless, 4.5W reverse wireless.
  • Connectivity: 5G; eSIM; Wi-Fi 7; BT 6.0, aptX HD; NFC.
  • Misc: Fingerprint reader (side-mounted); stereo speakers; Samsung DeX (desktop experience support), Ultra Wideband (UWB) support.

Samsung made sure to give it the same impeccable build quality and materials as the Z Fold8 Ultra. The two displays, while different in size and shape, promise very similar features and performance. Both are 120Hz LTPO AMOLED panels.

You are not giving up on any of the performance by going for the new form factor, though - the Snapdragon 8 Elite Gen 5 is still on board.

There rest of the specs also seem on par with the Ultra. The Z Fold8 gets stereo speakers, two 10MP selfie cameras and even the difference in battery capacity is negligible, with the Z Fold8 rocking a solid 4,800 mAh Si/C pack - only 200 mAh smaller than the pack inside the Z Fold8 Ultra. Not bad at all, especially considering that the Z Fold8 is about 15 grams lighter.

Samsung even went ahead and added its new optimized 45W charging on the Z Fold8. This is the fastest charging rating any Samsung foldable has had to date.

Unboxing

Seeing how this is a modern Samsung device, don't set your expectations too high in terms of accessories in the box. The Z Fold8 ships in what we would consider the standard Samsung box at this point. It is a two-piece cardboard affair with this kind of odd gray printing on the exterior, which starts to make more sense once you realize Samsung has eliminated all plastic from the packaging and prints everything in soy ink. Even so, the box is sturdy enough for safe transportation, and there is a cradle for the phone itself on the inside.

Accessories are pretty much non-existent. Samsung hasn't included a charger in the box for years now. There is no complimentary case either. All you get is a Type-C to Type-C cable. It is one of Samsung's passive, non-emarked 3A cables, so you don't even get a fancier 5A one. That said, we know for a fact that Samsung's modern 45W charging works just fine with a 3A cable, so there is that.

Design, build quality, handling

Design, build quality, handling

"Book-style" foldables are not exactly a new concept. Granted, with word of an upcoming iPhone Ultra in similar proportions on the horizon, manufacturers have unquestionably started looking at the concept once again. We already have the China-only Huawei Pura X Max and undoubtedly other major industry players will follow suit sooner rather than later on an accelerated timeline once the iPhone becomes a reality. That's just how the wheel usually turns.

And having spent some time with it we realize there is a lot to love about this form factor. In fact, this particular reviewer finds it much better than the Z Fold8 Ultra proportions. Perhaps it didn't really matter as much with early designs that couldn't quite make proper use of the folded state with far from borderless displays. Not that the Z Fold8 doesn't have pretty sizeable display borders as well, but the usability of the cover display is undeniable.

Yes, it's true, most of the modern online content does benefit from a taller display. You just scroll vertically most of the time to jump from post to post or further along in an article. However, there is something to be said about content scaling and display utilization. With its 10:16 aspect (or at least very close to that), the cover display is an all-around joy to use in pretty much every scenario. Social media looks great and in most cases spans a large portion of the display. 16:9 videos look great with minimal black bars. Reading is very pleasant, and the cover display is convenient to type on.

As for the main internal display, it is being marketed as 4:3 (though technically it is slightly off). We found that to be a perfectly-proportioned canvas for most activities as well. Your typical photo is 4:3, so that makes perfect sense. 16:9 video isn't half bad either. You would think that the Z Fold8 would lack usable space to properly "stretch and fit" a 16:9 video, but it's much better than the Z Fold8 Ultra as you can see from the photo.

Left:Samsung Galaxy Z Fold8 Ultra * Right: Samsung Galaxy Z Fold8

Beyond that, if you just happen to be a fan of, say, old anime or cartoons, 4:3 is kind of ideal. Reading, both comics and books, is pretty perfect too. And, while not as good as on the Ultra, there is enough space for two windows side by side for increased productivity. Gaming works surprisingly well too. Honestly the rather awkward camera viewfinder is probably the worst part.

The Z Fold8 is almost the same size as a passport, which is pretty standardized and the best proverbial "banana for reference" we can offer you right now. It is a very pocketable size.

Despite its different proportions, the Z Fold8 sticks surprisingly close to the overall design of the Z Fold family. That means flat sides and boxy corners, especially on the displays, which goes against modern design norms with aggressively curved corners.

Samsung Galaxy Z Fold8

The camera island design is also very "modern Samsung." It does look a bit weird with just two lenses on there, but it is definitely recognizable. All of the controls are exactly where you would expect them to be. The power button and volume rocker are well-positioned height-wise.

Just like on the Z Fold8 Ultra, Samsung opted for a side-mounted fingerprint reader. It is housed inside the power button. These capacitive readers have become really good at this point. This particular one is both snappy and accurate. We have no complaints with it.

The rest of the controls are pretty self-explanatory. Speaker positioning is the same as on the Z Fold8 Ultra. The two speakers sit on the same phone half, one on the top and the other on the bottom. This can be a bit awkward depending on how you hold the phone.

Samsung didn't skimp at all when it comes to construction and materials. The Z Fold8 is basically made in the exact same premium way as the Z Fold8 Ultra. You are looking at an Advanced Armor aluminum frame with a Gorilla Glass Victus 2 back and a Gorilla Glass Victus Ceramic 3 protecting the cover display.

The inner display is technically still covered by plastic. That's just how modern foldable displays work. That said, Samsung does boast about its new and improved "Flex Titanium" technology. It actually refers to a combination of a few things. The flexible Titanium plate from last year that sits underneath the display was carried over, and there is now a second flexible titanium plate in the AMOLED panel itself. It should make it more resilient, especially to pressure and impact, and Samsung also claims it makes the whole assembly thinner somehow.

Samsung also claims that the new construction is meant to "reduce crease visibility". In all fairness, our regular Z Fold8 unit has a far less visible crease than our Z Fold8 Ultra. That doesn't, however, mean that the crease is totally gone. It is still there, although you stop noticing it even faster than on most other foldables.

Samsung also says it has refined the hinge by adjusting the display tension and magnetic force to make unfolding feel "smoother, lighter and more natural". The company calls this the Easy Open-and-Close Mechanism, and says it draws inspiration from the Galaxy Z TriFold.

Left:Samsung Galaxy Z Fold8 * Right: Samsung Galaxy Z Fold8 Ultra

Compared to earlier Samsung hinge designs, the initial few millimeters of travel require noticeably less effort, with the magnets releasing more easily and the hinge offering virtually no resistance. That said, despite the improved feel, opening the phone one-handed is still difficult and remains impractical for most cases.

The Galaxy Z Fold8 has an IP48 ingress protection rating, matching the Fold8 Ultra. That means it should technically be able to survive a dip in up to 1.5 meters of fresh water for up to 30 minutes at a time. That's pretty much the best you can hope to get on a modern foldable.

Left:Samsung Galaxy Z Fold8 * Right: Samsung Galaxy Z Fold8 Ultra

We already mentioned this, but the Z Fold8 is quite easily pocketable. It measures 81.9 x 123.9 x 9.7 mm folded and 161.4 x 123.9 x 4.5 mm unfolded. That technically makes it notably thicker than the Z Fold8 Ultra, but the extra thickness is hardly noticeable in practice. If you have long enough fingers, grasping the phone closed with one hand and reaching pretty much every edge of the display is perfectly doable.

By the way, the cover display is anything but bezel-free. Samsung is certainly a step behind some competitors like Honor in this regard. It is hardly as bothersome on this shorter screen, but it is worth noting.

Finally, we would like to go through your options when it comes to colors. The Z Fold8 is available in the same "standard" Graphite and Cream as the rest of the 2026 Galaxy Z lineup. The hero color is called Lavender, and the Samsung.com exclusive color is Pistachio.

Displays

We already spoke about the display and how much we like its proportions, but let's now focus on performance. We'll start with the cover display first since it is certainly a lot different than the "tall and skinny" one on the Galaxy Z Fold8 Ultra as well as Z Fold devices of old.

It measures 5.5 inches in diagonal, which is technically smaller than the 6.5-inch panel on the Z Fold8 Ultra, though with a vastly different aspect ratio. The Z Fold8 Ultra has a FullHD+ resolution of 1080 x 2520 pixels on its outside display, which works out to a not-exactly-popular 3:7 aspect ratio. In contrast, the Z Fold8 cover display has a resolution of 1,248 x 1,972 pixels, which Samsung calls WUXGA+ and, at first glance, it does seem deceptively higher than the one on the Z Fold8 Ultra. In reality, both panels have very similar pixel density figures. The aspect ratio on the Z Fold8 outside display works out to an awkward 312:493, which is quite close to the 10:16 Samsung advertises.

As we already mentioned, we found the cover display to be quite versatile. Typing on it, in particular, was an excellent experience. Also, we particularly enjoyed scrolling social media like Instagram on this panel. Here are a few comparisons between the cover display of the Z Fold8 and an iPhone 17 Pro we had at hand. The iPhone 17 Pro certainly fits more content in a single screen, so there is that.

Samsung Galaxy Z Fold8 next to Apple iPhone 17 Pro

Let's look at some brightness numbers. The Z Fold8's cover display is about as bright as the one on the Z Fold7, which is interestingly slightly dimmer than the Z Fold6 cover display. The difference is about 100 nits, though, which is far from actually meaningful. In any case, the cover display on the Z Fold8 is perfectly comfortable to use outdoors even if it's not topping charts.

However, as far as we can tell, the cover display doesn't actually have a formal HDR certification as the inner one does. In practice, it does seem to recognize and react to HDR videos, boosting brightness in the process, so technically it kind of has HDR.

High refresh rate handling

The Z Fold8 has two Samsung AMOLED 2X panels. Both can refresh at up to 120Hz, and both have LTPO tech for proper dynamic refresh rate adjustment. This is yet another bit of display behavior that Samsung managed to unify, making life with twin displays a lot simpler overall.

Samsung has really simplified things at least as far as settings are concerned while introducing some more complexity and polish in the background. In our opinion, Samsung's current way of handling high refresh rate is among the best in the industry. Anyway, you get two settings to choose between - Standard and Adaptive. Despite your first inclination, Standard is not actually a fixed 60Hz mode. It is a 60Hz cap, essentially. The refresh rate is still dynamic. Samsung has excellent detection for what is actually happening on screen. For example, it will happily go down to 30Hz to accommodate 30fps video playback as well as 24Hz to specifically cater to 24fps video. That's not something you see that often.

In Adaptive mode, the general behavior is 120Hz while you are interacting with the display and 60Hz while idling. Some apps are limited to a 90Hz cap for one reason or another, but most can do 120Hz just fine.


Aspect ratio selection

Naturally, we tried a few test games that we know can push past the 60fps mark, and most of them indeed reached 120fps on the Z Fold8 with no issues. Also, on a side note, Samsung does have a special menu to select what aspect ratio you want to run any given app at on the main screen, so there is that as well, and it can definitely come in handy to combat some bugs in certain apps and games. Speaking of which, this is a good place as any to mention that while not all apps technically support multi-window and side-by-side operation, Samsung has had a Labs toggle for a while now that forces said behavior on any app and very few of them actually break or misbehave in our experience.

HDR

The Z Fold8 offers an excellent HDR experience. Certainly on the main display, but also, as far as we can tell, on the cover display. Even though the former seems to lack a formal HDR certification. On a decoder level, the phone has support for HDR10, HDR10+ and HLG. No Dolby Vision.

The phone has the highest possible Google Widevine L1 DRM certification, allowing apps like Netflix to offer up FullHD streams.

Battery life

The Galaxy Z Fold8 has a 4,800 mAh battery on board - not huge by modern standards, but not small either. As per Samsung's latest statement on the matter (and hopefully the final one), the Z Fold8 uses a Si/C battery just like the Z Fold8 Ultra. That helps engineers cram more capacity in the same volume among other things.

Anyway, we did our standardized testing and got nearly fourteen hours of Active Use Score out of the Z Fold8, which is quite decent for a foldable. It should comfortably last you through a whole day.

The Z Fold8 holds its own pretty well in our folded set of tests too. Sure, most of its competitors have it beat, but not by a lot.

Charging speed

The Galaxy Z Fold8 supports up to 45W wired charging. This is achieved through what Samsung calls and has historically called Super Fast Charging (SFC). More precisely, Super Fast Charging 2.0 is the one that goes up to 45W. It is based on USB Power Delivery (PD) with Programmable Power Supply (PPS) on top. In its current iteration, the protocol does not require a 5A cable to function properly. It works just fine with a 3A cable, like the one in the box.

45W is no small deal - it's actually a first on Samsung foldables as the Z Fold7 only accepts 25W charging.

Samsung promises the Z Fold8 can charge from dead to 63% in thirty minutes. We matched that and even exceeded it on our test, which was able to go from 1% to 39% in fifteen minutes and then to 65% in thirty. A full charge took just shy of seventy minutes, which is not amazing in the grand scheme of things, but still a marked improvement for a Samsung foldable.

Speakers - loudness and quality

The Galaxy Z Fold8 has a stereo speaker setup with two dedicated speakers quite similar in appearance and layout to that of the Z Fold8 Ultra. Both speakers are positioned on the same half of the phone, one on the top and the other on the bottom. One of these is pulling double duty as the earpiece.

During media playback, sound is certainly getting directed both up through the speaker hole and to the front above the cover display. Generally, this positioning works well when the phone is closed and held in landscape. When the phone is open in portrait, the arrangement works pretty well too. But any illusion of proper stereo separation and spacing falls apart in open landscape use. That's just the reality of the foldable form factor in general.

Left: Samsung Galaxy Z Fold8 Ultra * Right top: Samsung Galaxy Z Fold8 * Right bottom: Samsung Galaxy S26 Ultra

As mentioned, the speaker setup on the Z Fold8 looks a lot like that on the Z Fold8 Ultra. However, it's likely that Samsung had even less room to work with on the inside for proper resonance chambers. Hence, it comes as no surprise that the Z Fold8 is a tad quieter than the Z Fold7, which was already a downgrade compared to the Z Fold6 and even that one only managed a "Good" rating in our loudness test. Well, unfortunately, the Z Fold8 just misses the cutoff for "Good" territory and falls into "Average". To be clear, it is not terrible, but don't expect room-filling sound.

Quality is actually quite good, which frankly comes as no surprise. Samsung has great expertise when it comes to tuning smartphone speakers. Even working with limitations, the Z Fold8 sounds clear, especially where it matters most in mids. Highs rarely get distorted either. There is practically no bass, but that is expected as well.

There is Dolby Atmos support on board with plenty of presence as well as a full equalizer. Of course, Samsung included separate app sound, which remains one of our favorite features.

Use the Playback controls to listen to the phone sample recordings (best use headphones). We measure the average loudness of the speakers in LUFS. A lower absolute value means a louder sound. Connectivity

The Z Fold8 is a 5G device with a single physical nano-SIM slot at its disposal. It is not available with a second slot, nor do you get a microSD slot for that matter. Still, you can get up to two active network connections at a time through either one physical card and an eSIM or two eSIM subscriptions.

Location services include GPS, GALILEO, GLONASS, BDS and QZSS. Local connectivity includes tri-band Wi-Fi 7, which means you get access to the still uncongested 6 GHz band. Bluetooth is version 6.0 with LE and aptX HD support. There is, of course, NFC on board. No 3.5mm audio jack, no FM radio and no IR blaster, in case you were wondering.

The Type-C port on the Z Fold8 is backed up by what is marketed as a USB 3.2 data connection. That is technically true, but it is what is known as USB 3.2 Gen1, which is sometimes also referred to as USB 3.1 Gen 1 or USB 3.0. The important bit is that you get up to 5 Gbps data transfer speed. Of course, there is OTG/Host support, but beyond that the Z Fold8 also offers DP video output over USB Alt mode. That is not something you see every day. Samsung even has its industry-leading DeX system powering the second screen experience.

The Z Fold8 also has a pretty full set of onboard sensors. There is an STMicro lsm6dsv accelerometer and gyroscope combo, an akm ak0991x magnetometer and compass combo, and a SensorTek light sensor - actually two of those: one for the cover display and one for the inner one. There is also a bosch bmp5 barometer. The proximity sensor situation is a bit nuanced. There only seems to be one proximity sensor positioned near the earpiece on the cover display. It is a Samsung Palm Proximity Sensor version 2, which means that it is technically a hardware solution with an IR emitter and everything, but one that effectively only the phone can access on a low level and not any third-party apps. This is a pretty typical Samsung solution. It is interesting to note that there appears to be no proximity sensing at all for the main display. That does make sense since there really isn't a good way to make a call while the Z Fold8 is unfolded. There is not an easily accessible earpiece.

Benchmarks and performance

The Z Fold8 might not be the true flagship offer on the revamped Z Fold lineup, but Samsung certainly didn't penalize it in the compute department. The new form factor, just like its established, now Ultra sibling, runs on the Snapdragon 8 Elite Gen 5 chipset. And while it is true that said chip is a few months old at this point and bound for a next-gen replacement in a couple of months, these are kind of the realities of a modern foldable design and release cycle, so we can't reasonably be mad or anything.

In the CPU department, you are looking at an octa-core setup with two Oryon V3 Phoenix L cores, capable of whopping speeds up to 4.47 GHz. These are backed by six Oryon V3 Phoenix M cores, clocked at up to 3.62 GHz. The onboard GPU is an Adreno 840. Mind you, this is not your "ordinary" Snapdragon 8 Elite Gen 5 either, but rather the SM8850-1-AD variant, also known as the "for Galaxy" flavor. This means slightly higher clocks on both the CPU and GPU. We just hope this new form factor manages to keep things cool enough under pressure.

The chipset is paired with 12GB of LPDDR5X RAM pretty much across the board. A 16GB RAM variant with 1TB storage does exist, but it is hard to find and not exactly reasonable for most people with current memory prices as a whole. In any case, modern smartphone-tailored AI models seem to be happy with 12GB of RAM, and hopefully that remains true in the future as well. For storage, you can get 256GB, 512GB or the aforementioned 1TB. All are non-expandable. Samsung is using pretty fast (as per our testing) UFS storage chips, though we can't officially say whether these particular ones are UFS 4.0 or UFS 4.1.

Thermal-throttling

Looking at the benchmark figures as a whole, we can kind of assume that the Z Fold8 is less aggressively tuned for performance even though on paper the Snapdragon 8 Elite Gen 5 inside it appears to be operating at its "boosted" Samsung-exclusive clocks. We suspect the reason is this is a tough form factor to properly cool. After running some torture tests, it quickly became clear that under prolonged loads the Z Fold8 can lose around half of its initial burst performance. This sounds pretty bad, and we've certainly seen better behavior, but then again, even the S26 Ultra throttles to about 50% under the same load, so we can't expect miracles here.

Naturally, thermal behavior is a bit better when the phone is unfolded.

That said, the Z Fold8 does get notably hotter to the touch than the S26 Ultra. Specifically, the area on its back right underneath the inside bottom edge of the camera island where the chipset most likely resides. To be fair, our review unit never actually got uncomfortable to hold.

Camera - photo and video quality

The best cameras in this form factor

Samsung isn't really known for fitting industry-leading camera hardware on its conventional smartphones and the foldables typically come with even less impressive imaging chops. The Galaxy Z Fold8's newly introduced form factor adds further limitations in terms of size to an already space-constrained type of device, so we're not surprised to see just two cameras on its back - no telephoto, and mostly middle-of-the-road wide and ultrawide modules.

What we're looking at here is the main camera from the vanilla Galaxy S26 joined by the ultrawide from the S26 Ultra (or at least an identically specced one). It's not a bad setup, strictly speaking, but it's hardly an exciting one either.

As best as we can decode the info from hardware scanning apps, the main camera is based on the in-house GN3 sensor. The optical format is 1/1.56" (or 1/1.57" depending on where you're looking), so it's solid midrange stuff. It's got a 50MP nominal resolution and a 1.0µm pixel size.

The ultrawide uses a 50MP sensor with 0.7µm pixels - that much can be seen in the hardware info. Out of the long list of sensors that the phone reports, it's only the Sony IMX 858 that matches those numbers, so we're inclined to believe that's the one being used. That's different from the imager used on the Galaxy S26 Ultra (and S25 Ultra before it), which was a Samsung JN3, but the key specs should be the same. Or, of course, it's actually the JN3 here as well, and the reporting is flawed - wouldn't be the first time.

The selfie cameras use 10MP sensors with 1.12µm pixel size, but the exact model isn't listed (or we can't put our finger on it). Either way, the two cameras have different focal lengths - 23mm on the cover and a much wider 17mm on the internal one.

Video quality

The Z Fold8 comes with pretty much all the bells and whistles Samsung has to offer in terms of video recording capabilities - within the limitations of its actual camera hardware, of course. In the regular Video mode, the phone can record up to 4K60 with all four of its cameras (including the two 'selfie' cameras), and you also get 8K30 on the two rear cameras.

The Pro video mode adds a whole bunch of other frame rates, including 24, 25, 48, 50, 100, and 120fps modes for 4K (the 100 and 120fps are only for the rear cameras), and 24fps for 8K.

The out-of-the-box setting for the video codec is h.265 (HEVC), but you can opt for the older and less efficient h.264 codec. The Folds also get the Samsung-developed pro-grade APV codec (Advanced Professional Video) - the S26 Ultra had that, but not the plain S26s. It enables high-quality perceptually lossless encoding resulting in substantial file sizes (1.5GB for a minute of 1080p30), which is why Samsung recommends recording to external media in this case.

There's an option for 10-bit HDR10+ recording, as well as a Log option that can be enabled for both Pro video and regular Video mode, complete with a selection of pre-loaded LUTs (Standard, Blockbuster, Coming-of-age, Romance and Thriller). In Pro mode you also get finer control over focus and exposure (complete with zebras and false color aids), plus mic directionality settings and audio level indicators.

Video stabilization is available in all modes on all cameras, and you can disable it in settings if you have other means of ensuring stability. The Horizon lock stabilization mode is also available on the Z Fold8, but Samsung is no longer making a big deal out of it like they did at the S26 series announcement.

Our verdict

The Galaxy Z Fold8 is one of those devices whose appeal hinges largely on whether its unusual proportions speak to you. That will inevitably be a matter of personal preference, but after spending time with it, we think this form factor makes a lot of sense for many - perhaps a much larger crowd than the productivity-focused traditional Galaxy Fold.

The passport-like dimensions make the Z Fold8 impressively compact and pocketable, while the wider cover screen is genuinely pleasant to use rather than merely serving as a secondary display for quick tasks. Typing, reading, watching videos and browsing social media all work wonderfully on it. Open the phone, and the 4:3 internal panel provides another remarkably versatile canvas for photos, books, comics, games and side-by-side multitasking. The camera viewfinder is about the only situation where the proportions become slightly awkward.

Samsung has not treated the regular Z Fold8 like a second-rate member of the family. Its construction is every bit as premium as that of the Z Fold8 Ultra, complete with excellent materials, a reassuring hinge and IP48 protection. Both LTPO AMOLED displays are sharp, bright and superbly controlled, while the Snapdragon 8 Elite Gen 5 delivers flagship-grade performance. Sustained loads do result in substantial throttling and quite a bit of heat, but everyday performance is beyond reproach.

One UI remains arguably the most complete foldable software experience around, especially when it comes to multitasking and making best use of the larger display. One UI 9 does not reinvent Samsung's interface, but it adds further polish, plenty of features and some interesting new Galaxy AI functionality. The promise of seven major OS upgrades makes the long-term proposition even stronger. DeX, USB video output, UWB and the extensive connectivity package are welcome additions as well.

Battery life is quite respectable and should comfortably cover a full day. The new 45W charging is a meaningful improvement too, even if Samsung remains well behind some competitors in absolute charging speed. Speaker quality is good, though loudness is merely average and the positioning does little for stereo separation when the phone is unfolded in landscape.

The cameras are the one area where the Z Fold8 makes a notable compromise. The physical constraints of the form factor probably explain the decision, but they do not make it any less of a drawback. The autofocusing ultrawide is very capable, the selfie options are unusually versatile, and Samsung provides an exceptionally complete video feature set. However, the main camera is not flagship-grade, particularly in low light, and the complete absence of a dedicated telephoto is difficult to overlook on a phone this expensive.

And expensive the Galaxy Z Fold8 certainly is. At around €2,000 for the base model, you can buy practically any conventional flagship or choose among several technically more ambitious traditional foldables. Samsung's own Z Fold8 Ultra is not much more expensive either and adds a dedicated telephoto camera.

However, none of those devices delivers quite the same experience. For the moment, the Galaxy Z Fold8 occupies a niche largely of its own, combining a compact body, a fully usable cover screen and a versatile tablet-like internal display without compromising the rest of the flagship experience in any major way.

We like the Galaxy Z Fold8 a lot. Its proportions will certainly help bring a lot more people to the foldable camp. Other than its underwhelming camera hardware, there is nothing really wrong with it. And whila it remains in a league of its own, it is difficult not to recommend.

And having spent some time with it we realize there is a lot to love about this form factor. In fact, this particular reviewer finds it much better than the Z Fold8 Ultra proportions. Not that the Z Fold8 doesn't have pretty sizeable display borders as well, but the usability of the cover display is undeniable.

However, there is something to be said about content scaling and display utilization. With its 10:16 aspect (or at least very close to that), the cover display is an all-around joy to use in pretty much every scenario. Social media looks great and in most cases spans a large portion of the display. 16:9 videos look great with minimal black bars. As for the main internal display, it is being marketed as 4:3 (though technically it is slightly off). We found that to be a perfectly-proportioned canvas for most activities as well. Your typical photo is 4:3, so that makes perfect sense. 16:9 video isn't half bad either. The flexible Titanium plate from last year that sits underneath the display was carried over, and there is now a second flexible titanium plate in the AMOLED panel itself. It should make it more resilient, especially to pressure and impact, and Samsung also claims it makes the whole assembly thinner somehow.

High refresh rate handling

The Z Fold8 has two Samsung AMOLED 2X panels. Both can refresh at up to 120Hz, and both have LTPO tech for proper dynamic refresh rate adjustment. This is yet another bit of display behavior that Samsung managed to unify, making life with twin displays a lot simpler overall.

Samsung has really simplified things at least as far as settings are concerned while introducing some more complexity and polish in the background. In our opinion, Samsung's current way of handling high refresh rate is among the best in the industry. Anyway, you get two settings to choose between - Standard and Adaptive. Despite your first inclination, Standard is not actually a fixed 60Hz mode. It is a 60Hz cap, essentially. The refresh rate is still dynamic. Samsung has excellent detection for what is actually happening on screen. For example, it will happily go down to 30Hz to accommodate 30fps video playback as well as 24Hz to specifically cater to 24fps video. That's not something you see that often.

In Adaptive mode, the general behavior is 120Hz while you are interacting with the display and 60Hz while idling. Some apps are limited to a 90Hz cap for one reason or another, but most can do 120Hz just fine.


Aspect ratio selection

Naturally, we tried a few test games that we know can push past the 60fps mark, and most of them indeed reached 120fps on the Z Fold8 with no issues. Also, on a side note, Samsung does have a special menu to select what aspect ratio you want to run any given app at on the main screen, so there is that as well, and it can definitely come in handy to combat some bugs in certain apps and games. Speaking of which, this is a good place as any to mention that while not all apps technically support multi-window and side-by-side operation, Samsung has had a Labs toggle for a while now that forces said behavior on any app and very few of them actually break or misbehave in our experience.

HDR

The Z Fold8 offers an excellent HDR experience. Certainly on the main display, but also, as far as we can tell, on the cover display. Even though the former seems to lack a formal HDR certification. On a decoder level, the phone has support for HDR10, HDR10+ and HLG. No Dolby Vision.

The phone has the highest possible Google Widevine L1 DRM certification, allowing apps like Netflix to offer up FullHD streams.

Charging speed

The Galaxy Z Fold8 supports up to 45W wired charging. This is achieved through what Samsung calls and has historically called Super Fast Charging (SFC). More precisely, Super Fast Charging 2.0 is the one that goes up to 45W. It is based on USB Power Delivery (PD) with Programmable Power Supply (PPS) on top. In its current iteration, the protocol does not require a 5A cable to function properly. It works just fine with a 3A cable, like the one in the box.

45W is no small deal - it's actually a first on Samsung foldables as the Z Fold7 only accepts 25W charging.

Samsung promises the Z Fold8 can charge from dead to 63% in thirty minutes. We matched that and even exceeded it on our test, which was able to go from 1% to 39% in fifteen minutes and then to 65% in thirty. A full charge took just shy of seventy minutes, which is not amazing in the grand scheme of things, but still a marked improvement for a Samsung foldable.

Speakers - loudness and quality

The Galaxy Z Fold8 has a stereo speaker setup with two dedicated speakers quite similar in appearance and layout to that of the Z Fold8 Ultra. Both speakers are positioned on the same half of the phone, one on the top and the other on the bottom. One of these is pulling double duty as the earpiece.

During media playback, sound is certainly getting directed both up through the speaker hole and to the front above the cover display. Generally, this positioning works well when the phone is closed and held in landscape. When the phone is open in portrait, the arrangement works pretty well too. But any illusion of proper stereo separation and spacing falls apart in open landscape use. That's just the reality of the foldable form factor in general.

Left: Samsung Galaxy Z Fold8 Ultra * Right top: Samsung Galaxy Z Fold8 * Right bottom: Samsung Galaxy S26 Ultra

As mentioned, the speaker setup on the Z Fold8 looks a lot like that on the Z Fold8 Ultra. However, it's likely that Samsung had even less room to work with on the inside for proper resonance chambers. Hence, it comes as no surprise that the Z Fold8 is a tad quieter than the Z Fold7, which was already a downgrade compared to the Z Fold6 and even that one only managed a "Good" rating in our loudness test. Well, unfortunately, the Z Fold8 just misses the cutoff for "Good" territory and falls into "Average". To be clear, it is not terrible, but don't expect room-filling sound.

Quality is actually quite good, which frankly comes as no surprise. Samsung has great expertise when it comes to tuning smartphone speakers. Even working with limitations, the Z Fold8 sounds clear, especially where it matters most in mids. Highs rarely get distorted either. There is practically no bass, but that is expected as well.

There is Dolby Atmos support on board with plenty of presence as well as a full equalizer. Of course, Samsung included separate app sound, which remains one of our favorite features.

Use the Playback controls to listen to the phone sample recordings (best use headphones). We measure the average loudness of the speakers in LUFS. A lower absolute value means a louder sound. A look at the frequency response chart will tell you how far off the ideal "0db" flat line is the reproduction of the bass, treble, and mid frequencies. You can add more phones to compare how they differ. The scores and ratings are not comparable with our older loudspeaker test. Learn more about how we test here.

Connectivity

The Z Fold8 is a 5G device with a single physical nano-SIM slot at its disposal. It is not available with a second slot, nor do you get a microSD slot for that matter. Still, you can get up to two active network connections at a time through either one physical card and an eSIM or two eSIM subscriptions.

Location services include GPS, GALILEO, GLONASS, BDS and QZSS. Local connectivity includes tri-band Wi-Fi 7, which means you get access to the still uncongested 6 GHz band. Bluetooth is version 6.0 with LE and aptX HD support. There is, of course, NFC on board. No 3.5mm audio jack, no FM radio and no IR blaster, in case you were wondering.

The Type-C port on the Z Fold8 is backed up by what is marketed as a USB 3.2 data connection. That is technically true, but it is what is known as USB 3.2 Gen1, which is sometimes also referred to as USB 3.1 Gen 1 or USB 3.0. The important bit is that you get up to 5 Gbps data transfer speed. Of course, there is OTG/Host support, but beyond that the Z Fold8 also offers DP video output over USB Alt mode. That is not something you see every day. Samsung even has its industry-leading DeX system powering the second screen experience.

The Z Fold8 also has a pretty full set of onboard sensors. There is an STMicro lsm6dsv accelerometer and gyroscope combo, an akm ak0991x magnetometer and compass combo, and a SensorTek light sensor - actually two of those: one for the cover display and one for the inner one. There is also a bosch bmp5 barometer. The proximity sensor situation is a bit nuanced. There only seems to be one proximity sensor positioned near the earpiece on the cover display. It is a Samsung Palm Proximity Sensor version 2, which means that it is technically a hardware solution with an IR emitter and everything, but one that effectively only the phone can access on a low level and not any third-party apps. This is a pretty typical Samsung solution. It is interesting to note that there appears to be no proximity sensing at all for the main display. That does make sense since there really isn't a good way to make a call while the Z Fold8 is unfolded. There is not an easily accessible earpiece.

Benchmarks and performance

The Z Fold8 might not be the true flagship offer on the revamped Z Fold lineup, but Samsung certainly didn't penalize it in the compute department. The new form factor, just like its established, now Ultra sibling, runs on the Snapdragon 8 Elite Gen 5 chipset. And while it is true that said chip is a few months old at this point and bound for a next-gen replacement in a couple of months, these are kind of the realities of a modern foldable design and release cycle, so we can't reasonably be mad or anything.

In the CPU department, you are looking at an octa-core setup with two Oryon V3 Phoenix L cores, capable of whopping speeds up to 4.47 GHz. These are backed by six Oryon V3 Phoenix M cores, clocked at up to 3.62 GHz. The onboard GPU is an Adreno 840. Mind you, this is not your "ordinary" Snapdragon 8 Elite Gen 5 either, but rather the SM8850-1-AD variant, also known as the "for Galaxy" flavor. This means slightly higher clocks on both the CPU and GPU. We just hope this new form factor manages to keep things cool enough under pressure.

The chipset is paired with 12GB of LPDDR5X RAM pretty much across the board. A 16GB RAM variant with 1TB storage does exist, but it is hard to find and not exactly reasonable for most people with current memory prices as a whole. In any case, modern smartphone-tailored AI models seem to be happy with 12GB of RAM, and hopefully that remains true in the future as well. For storage, you can get 256GB, 512GB or the aforementioned 1TB. All are non-expandable. Samsung is using pretty fast (as per our testing) UFS storage chips, though we can't officially say whether these particular ones are UFS 4.0 or UFS 4.1.

Let's look at some actual performance numbers starting with GeekBench 6. For some reason, the Z Fold8 is a bit inconsistent in its multi-core scores in particular, even though we make sure to let the phone cool down between runs. The single-core numbers are a lot more consistent. Anyway, comparing it to the likes of the Galaxy S26 Ultra, we can see very similar output, but just a tad lower. We made sure to throw a few on the popular chipsets in the chart as well, mainly for reference.

This overall trend carries over to AnTuTu testing as well. That is to say that the Z Fold8 is putting out very formidable performance numbers, but not quite maximizing the Snapdragon 8 Elite Gen 5 for Galaxy's potential. And the same is generally true of 3DMark and its GPU testing too.

Thermal-throttling

Looking at the benchmark figures as a whole, we can kind of assume that the Z Fold8 is less aggressively tuned for performance even though on paper the Snapdragon 8 Elite Gen 5 inside it appears to be operating at its "boosted" Samsung-exclusive clocks. We suspect the reason is this is a tough form factor to properly cool. After running some torture tests, it quickly became clear that under prolonged loads the Z Fold8 can lose around half of its initial burst performance. This sounds pretty bad, and we've certainly seen better behavior, but then again, even the S26 Ultra throttles to about 50% under the same load, so we can't expect miracles here.

Naturally, thermal behavior is a bit better when the phone is unfolded.

That said, the Z Fold8 does get notably hotter to the touch than the S26 Ultra. Specifically, the area on its back right underneath the inside bottom edge of the camera island where the chipset most likely resides. To be fair, our review unit never actually got uncomfortable to hold.

Camera - photo and video quality

The best cameras in this form factor

Samsung isn't really known for fitting industry-leading camera hardware on its conventional smartphones and the foldables typically come with even less impressive imaging chops. The Galaxy Z Fold8's newly introduced form factor adds further limitations in terms of size to an already space-constrained type of device, so we're not surprised to see just two cameras on its back - no telephoto, and mostly middle-of-the-road wide and ultrawide modules.

What we're looking at here is the main camera from the vanilla Galaxy S26 joined by the ultrawide from the S26 Ultra (or at least an identically specced one). It's not a bad setup, strictly speaking, but it's hardly an exciting one either.

As best as we can decode the info from hardware scanning apps, the main camera is based on the in-house GN3 sensor. The optical format is 1/1.56" (or 1/1.57" depending on where you're looking), so it's solid midrange stuff. It's got a 50MP nominal resolution and a 1.0µm pixel size.

The ultrawide uses a 50MP sensor with 0.7µm pixels - that much can be seen in the hardware info. Out of the long list of sensors that the phone reports, it's only the Sony IMX 858 that matches those numbers, so we're inclined to believe that's the one being used. That's different from the imager used on the Galaxy S26 Ultra (and S25 Ultra before it), which was a Samsung JN3, but the key specs should be the same. Or, of course, it's actually the JN3 here as well, and the reporting is flawed - wouldn't be the first time.

The selfie cameras use 10MP sensors with 1.12µm pixel size, but the exact model isn't listed (or we can't put our finger on it). Either way, the two cameras have different focal lengths - 23mm on the cover and a much wider 17mm on the internal one.

  • Wide (main): 50MP Samsung ISOCELL GN3 (S5KGN3, 1/1.56", 1.0µm-2.0µm); 23mm, f/1.8, OIS, dual-pixel PDAF (9.5cm - ∞); 8K30/4K120 video recording.
  • Ultrawide: 50MP Sony IMX 858 (1/2.51", 0.7µm-1.4µm); 13mm, f/1.9, multi-directional PDAF; 8K30/4K120.
  • Cover camera: 10MP (1/3.52", 1.12µm); 24mm, f/2.2, fixed focus; 4K60.
  • Internal camera: 10MP (1/3.52", 1.12µm); 18mm, f/2.2, fixed focus; 4K60.

When it comes to using this new style of foldable as a camera, the more squarish cover display means that the shutter release button is actually on top of the viewfinder image, as opposed to being outside of the live preview - you may not be able to tap to focus/adjust exposure on one side of the frame because there are UI elements there. Maybe you won't notice it at all, or maybe it will be a bit of a nuisance every now and then.

Photo quality verdict

The Galaxy Z Fold8 pairs a midrange main camera with what is Samsung's best ultrawide, which itself is more like midrange material in absolute terms. The main camera is generally good in daylight and a bit less convincing but still alright at night, and the 2x zoom results are actually quite good given the hardware. The ultrawide maintains a somewhat higher standard in its own context, with great daytime shots and stable low-light performance. Each of the in-display selfie cameras is solid in its own right, though you'll naturally get better selfies if you go the extra mile and shoot with the device unfolded.

Video quality

The Z Fold8 comes with pretty much all the bells and whistles Samsung has to offer in terms of video recording capabilities - within the limitations of its actual camera hardware, of course. In the regular Video mode, the phone can record up to 4K60 with all four of its cameras (including the two 'selfie' cameras), and you also get 8K30 on the two rear cameras.

The Pro video mode adds a whole bunch of other frame rates, including 24, 25, 48, 50, 100, and 120fps modes for 4K (the 100 and 120fps are only for the rear cameras), and 24fps for 8K.

The out-of-the-box setting for the video codec is h.265 (HEVC), but you can opt for the older and less efficient h.264 codec. The Folds also get the Samsung-developed pro-grade APV codec (Advanced Professional Video) - the S26 Ultra had that, but not the plain S26s. It enables high-quality perceptually lossless encoding resulting in substantial file sizes (1.5GB for a minute of 1080p30), which is why Samsung recommends recording to external media in this case.

There's an option for 10-bit HDR10+ recording, as well as a Log option that can be enabled for both Pro video and regular Video mode, complete with a selection of pre-loaded LUTs (Standard, Blockbuster, Coming-of-age, Romance and Thriller). In Pro mode you also get finer control over focus and exposure (complete with zebras and false color aids), plus mic directionality settings and audio level indicators.

Video stabilization is available in all modes on all cameras, and you can disable it in settings if you have other means of ensuring stability. The Horizon lock stabilization mode is also available on the Z Fold8, but Samsung is no longer making a big deal out of it like they did at the S26 series announcement.

Daytime video quality is solid overall, while in the dark the ultrawide is probably a touch more competitive than the main camera.

The competition

Until Apple eventually drops the rumored iPhone Ultra and more of the popular manufacturers follow suit with their own take on this particular form factor, the Galaxy Z Fold8 stands mostly on its own. Sure, there are exotic devices out there like the Huawei Pura X Max, but those are exclusive to China. In the mainstream, the Z Fold8 is sort of a unique offering for now.

Our verdict

The Galaxy Z Fold8 is one of those devices whose appeal hinges largely on whether its unusual proportions speak to you. That will inevitably be a matter of personal preference, but after spending time with it, we think this form factor makes a lot of sense for many - perhaps a much larger crowd than the productivity-focused traditional Galaxy Fold.

The passport-like dimensions make the Z Fold8 impressively compact and pocketable, while the wider cover screen is genuinely pleasant to use rather than merely serving as a secondary display for quick tasks. Typing, reading, watching videos and browsing social media all work wonderfully on it. Open the phone, and the 4:3 internal panel provides another remarkably versatile canvas for photos, books, comics, games and side-by-side multitasking. The camera viewfinder is about the only situation where the proportions become slightly awkward.

Samsung has not treated the regular Z Fold8 like a second-rate member of the family. Its construction is every bit as premium as that of the Z Fold8 Ultra, complete with excellent materials, a reassuring hinge and IP48 protection. Both LTPO AMOLED displays are sharp, bright and superbly controlled, while the Snapdragon 8 Elite Gen 5 delivers flagship-grade performance. Sustained loads do result in substantial throttling and quite a bit of heat, but everyday performance is beyond reproach.

One UI remains arguably the most complete foldable software experience around, especially when it comes to multitasking and making best use of the larger display. One UI 9 does not reinvent Samsung's interface, but it adds further polish, plenty of features and some interesting new Galaxy AI functionality. The promise of seven major OS upgrades makes the long-term proposition even stronger. DeX, USB video output, UWB and the extensive connectivity package are welcome additions as well.

Battery life is quite respectable and should comfortably cover a full day. The new 45W charging is a meaningful improvement too, even if Samsung remains well behind some competitors in absolute charging speed. Speaker quality is good, though loudness is merely average and the positioning does little for stereo separation when the phone is unfolded in landscape.

The cameras are the one area where the Z Fold8 makes a notable compromise. The physical constraints of the form factor probably explain the decision, but they do not make it any less of a drawback. The autofocusing ultrawide is very capable, the selfie options are unusually versatile, and Samsung provides an exceptionally complete video feature set. However, the main camera is not flagship-grade, particularly in low light, and the complete absence of a dedicated telephoto is difficult to overlook on a phone this expensive.

And expensive the Galaxy Z Fold8 certainly is. At around €2,000 for the base model, you can buy practically any conventional flagship or choose among several technically more ambitious traditional foldables. Samsung's own Z Fold8 Ultra is not much more expensive either and adds a dedicated telephoto camera.

However, none of those devices delivers quite the same experience. For the moment, the Galaxy Z Fold8 occupies a niche largely of its own, combining a compact body, a fully usable cover screen and a versatile tablet-like internal display without compromising the rest of the flagship experience in any major way.

We like the Galaxy Z Fold8 a lot. Its proportions will certainly help bring a lot more people to the foldable camp. Other than its underwhelming camera hardware, there is nothing really wrong with it. And whila it remains in a league of its own, it is difficult not to recommend.
